# Journaling: evidence and limits

Daily close is an optional way to record your own words, choose a mood, and decide what to do with one Inbox draft. You can skip any question or stop. Bearing does not promise a wellbeing benefit.

## Expressive writing

Pennebaker & Beall (1986) studied structured writing about traumatic experiences, including thoughts and feelings. This was a particular experimental exercise, not a test of Bearing's Daily close or a daily gratitude quota. [Confronting a traumatic event](https://doi.org/10.1037/0021-843X.95.3.274).

Frattaroli's (2006) meta-analysis combined 146 randomized disclosure studies. It found a small average effect (r = .075), with results varying by study conditions. An average effect cannot tell us whether writing will help a particular person. [Experimental disclosure and its moderators](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.132.6.823).

## Gratitude

Emmons & McCullough (2003) compared gratitude lists with other writing conditions in three studies. Some wellbeing outcomes improved, especially positive affect; not every outcome did. [Counting blessings versus burdens](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.84.2.377).

Davis et al. (2016) found mixed results across outcomes and comparison groups. Wellbeing effects were small relative to alternative activities (d = .17), and gratitude interventions did not outperform psychologically active comparisons. That limits claims that gratitude itself is uniquely helpful. [Thankful for the little things](https://doi.org/10.1037/cou0000107).

## What this does not establish

These sources do not establish that this app, its template, a gratitude quota, or completing every day improves health or mood. They do not justify inferring a person's mood, diagnosing from Journal text, or treating Daily close as clinical care. You do not need to disclose difficult experiences, find a positive spin, or finish a template. Record only what you choose; skipping is valid.
